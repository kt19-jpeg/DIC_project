

2.7 Surprise Findings

Surprise Finding 1: Drug Correlations Reflected Population Size, Not Drug-Specific Patterns

We expected different drug types to exhibit distinct state-level patterns. However, the correlation matrix showed strong correlations across most substances (0.72–0.85 range)  ￼. Rather than revealing unique drug-specific dynamics, the correlations largely reflected population size — larger states had higher death counts across all substances. This shifted our understanding that raw counts obscure structural differences and that per-capita normalization would be required to isolate substance-specific trends.



Surprise Finding 2: Indicator Definitions Overlap, Making Rankings Structurally Invalid

We attempted to rank drug indicators by national average death count to determine which substances were most harmful. However, many indicators are nested within broader ICD code categories (e.g., heroin and methadone are included within “Opioids”)  ￼. This means categories are not mutually exclusive, and direct comparisons result in double counting. What appeared to be a straightforward ranking exercise turned out to be structurally invalid. This changed our approach by forcing us to treat indicators as overlapping classifications rather than independent categories.


Surprise Finding 3: Puerto Rico Lacks Sufficient Granular Data for Comparative Analysis

We initially considered Puerto Rico as a compelling comparative case due to its distinct healthcare system and demographics. However, 61.2% of Puerto Rico’s Data Values are missing  ￼, with certain drug indicators missing over 90% of observations (e.g., heroin 91%, psychostimulants 92%)  ￼. Notably, “Number of Deaths” is 100% missing while “Number of Drug Overdose Deaths” is fully present  ￼, revealing structural inconsistency in reporting. This surprised us and led to the conclusion that Puerto Rico cannot be reliably used for granular state-level comparison in this dataset.
